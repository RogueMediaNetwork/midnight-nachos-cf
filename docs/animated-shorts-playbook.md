# Animated Shorts Playbook

Distilled from Jack Craig's 30-day challenge video: cloning the format of viral
Minecraft-animation Shorts into a new niche (football) with a hired animator.
Final result: 37,000 subscribers and 3.4M views in 30 days from ~6 uploads, with
two shorts crossing 1.6M+ views each. Channel hit the first monetization
milestone but NOT the $5,000/month goal within 30 days.

This doc captures every tactic from the video, plus honest caveats at the end.
It is the reference for our own market research and channel launch.

---

## 1. The core strategy (the one idea everything hangs on)

**Proven Format Transfer.** Find a niche where a format is already proven viral
at massive scale (Minecraft animation Shorts pulling 46M–128M views), then port
that exact format — same story beats, same pacing, same payoff structure, often
the same soundtrack — into a different, less-saturated niche with a huge
audience (he chose football).

Why it works, in his words: a short that reached 80M views has something
inherently viral in it (hook, pacing, editing, payoff). You don't need to know
*which* element it was — you transplant the whole structure and only re-skin
the surface (characters, objects, setting).

Rule of thumb he follows: **don't compete in the proven niche itself** — it's
dominated by established channels uploading similar content. Take the proven
topics *out* of it.

## 2. Niche selection checklist

1. Find a niche with consistent multi-million-view Shorts (filter channels by
   recent shorts, look for repeated viral hits — not one-offs).
2. Do **competitor analysis before committing** (he admits he did it too late):
   - List every channel doing your target combo (he found only 3 doing football
     animation).
   - Grade each: still active in the niche? Upload frequency? Quality? Topic
     creativity?
   - Red flags in competitors are opportunities: one had quit the niche, one
     uploaded too frequently with low quality, only one (Skippers, weekly,
     high-quality) was a real competitor — and even they weren't dominant.
3. Target audience must be enormous (football = "most popular topic in the
   world").
4. Success criteria: if early uploads get flat/no traction, either the niche is
   saturated or there's no audience — move on.
5. His repeated claim: niche + topic selection is the #1 lever. It's what lets
   you go viral within a handful of uploads instead of grinding 30+.

## 3. Channel setup

- **Account warm-up:** use an account that has been active for at least 1 week
  before the first upload (his was from 2017; age beyond a week doesn't matter).
- **Naming:** generate blended-niche names with ChatGPT (his pick: "FootMotion"
  = football + animation). If taken on YouTube, append "real" or similar.
- **Assets:** profile picture = channel name as text; banner = "subscribe".
  Spend ~5 minutes total — **channel art does not affect performance.**
- Expect the zero-subscriber dashboard to feel discouraging; ignore it.

## 4. Production pipeline (division of labor)

- **Owner/strategist (him):** research viral references, write the storyboard
  (scene design + retention-based story), brief the animator, edit sound, upload,
  monitor analytics. Storyboard + research ≈ 25 min. Sound/edit pass ≈ 10–15 min
  in Premiere Pro (sound effects + music track).
- **Animator (partner, not employee):** produces the animation from the
  storyboard. Met through his coaching community; formed a revenue partnership
  rather than paying per video.
- Storyboard spec per short: general scene design + story with retention beats,
  modeled directly on a specific reference short.
- Story structure he ports from the references: **rising action → climax
  (a character fails) → resolution/payoff (last character gets revenge /
  transforms / wins)**. Simple, wordless, universal.
- For re-skinned shorts: replace characters with niche characters (footballers),
  replace relevant objects, swap the scene (football stadium), keep pacing and
  even the soundtrack from the source short.

## 5. Upload settings (deliberately minimal)

- **Title:** short description of the content + emojis (e.g. "Help Ronaldo save
  Messi"). Nothing clever.
- **Description:** restate the title + `#shorts`.
- **Audience flag:** always "not made for kids" (avoid being buried in YouTube
  Kids).
- **Tags:** a few niche-relevant ones; matter most on the first upload.
- Everything else default. "You really don't need to worry about optimizing
  upload settings."
- **Habit:** always watch the short back in the upload preview player — he
  repeatedly catches errors there.
- Export at the best quality settings available.

## 6. The algorithm model he operates by

- **First upload = algorithm test.** Its job is to let YouTube find the audience
  that engages with your content. Don't over-invest in it. Virality typically
  starts on upload 2–4.
- **Watch hourly view "candles"** (real-time analytics):
  - Consecutively rising hourly views → viral breakout forming; do nothing,
    let it run.
  - Sustained flat or declining candles → the short has flatlined; now it's
    safe to upload the next one.
- **One short at a time.** YouTube pushes one short per small channel. Uploading
  while a short is being pushed can strand the new upload at ~100 views
  (his 4th short: 121 views while the 3rd was at 900K). Give shorts "time to
  breathe."
- **Time-of-day sensitivity** appears once views are large — his channel pulled
  27K views/hour at night vs. a slowdown mid-morning. Flat ≠ dead if it's the
  slow part of the day.
- **Longer shorts are an advantage** (more total watch time — "the most
  important metric on every platform") — but a proven 26-second reference at
  46M views overrides that concern. Proven format beats theoretical
  optimization.
- **Benchmark upload-vs-upload:** compare first-24-hour views to your previous
  shorts' first-24-hours to project the ceiling (14K in <24h vs. 4.8K for the
  prior one → strong signal).
- Growth pattern when it works: modest first push (2.5K views/75 subs day 1) →
  second push after ~24h → exponential phase (10K+ views/hour) days later.
  Virality shows up on a delay.
- New-viewer share (the "new viewers" line in analytics) is the health metric —
  growth shouldn't depend on one viral short recycling the same audience.

## 7. Troubleshooting dead uploads (his exact debug tree)

When a new short sticks at ~100 views despite a proven process:

1. **Check traffic sources.** Shorts feed should dominate. If most views come
   from "channel pages," YouTube isn't pushing you into the feed.
2. **Hypothesize causes:** (a) content flagged by moderation AI (suggestive
   scene), (b) copyrighted sound effects from movies/TV (explicitly against
   Shorts policy), (c) YouTube busy pushing your other viral short.
3. **Fix what's fixable, then re-upload once** to isolate the variable.
4. **Never re-upload an unchanged short — that risks a shadowban.** Always
   change something material (he rebuilt the full soundtrack and had the
   animator redo the flagged scene).
5. If it still dies, **wait for the viral short to cool down** (watch the
   candles), then upload a cheap "canary" short you don't care about, purely to
   test whether the channel still gets feed pushes. His canary got pushed within
   2 hours → channel fine → then re-launch the short you believe in.
6. His shelved short, re-released with patience: 1.6M views. The process
   failure was pacing, not the content.

## 8. Mindset / meta rules

- Be methodical, not spray-and-pray: every upload is either a proven-format
  port or a controlled test with a hypothesis.
- Don't let emotions swing with the analytics — he was "extremely discouraged"
  at 121 views on a short that later did 1.6M.
- Early-stage shorts channels are surprisingly passive: most days are waiting
  and monitoring, not producing. Over-uploading actively hurts.
- Consistent cadence only after the channel is established; early on, cadence
  is dictated by the candles, not a calendar.
- You don't need an animator or outsourcing to start — his previous channels
  were solo. The niche/format selection is the transferable part.

## 10. Honest caveats before we copy this

- **The video is a sales funnel.** The last third is an ad for his ~90-day
  coaching program, and the student results (Carlos, Aaron) are unverifiable
  testimonials. The tactics are still observable and testable, but treat every
  claim as marketing until our own data confirms it.
- **Survivorship bias:** we see the challenge that worked. Failed challenge
  videos don't get published.
- **Revenue was NOT achieved in the window.** 3.4M views ≠ $5K/month; Shorts
  RPM is low (typically ~$0.05–0.20 per 1K Shorts views), so even 3.4M views is
  roughly a few hundred dollars. The real monetization story (RPM, sponsor
  deals, products) is the gap this video never fills — our market research must
  cover it.
- **Copying formats + soundtracks wholesale** brushes against reuse/copyright
  policy (he got burned once by movie sound effects). We should port structure,
  not assets.
- **The animator was free (partnership).** Our cost model must price animation
  realistically (partner rev-share, freelancer per short, or AI/self-produced).
- The "account active 1 week" and "shadowban" rules are community folklore, not
  documented YouTube policy — cheap to comply with, but don't over-index.

## 11. What this means for our channel (inputs needed)

To run this playbook we must choose, in order:

1. **Source niche** — an animation-Shorts niche with repeat 10M+ view formats
   to harvest (Minecraft is the canonical one; others exist).
2. **Target niche** — huge audience, weak animated-content competition
   (≤ a handful of active channels, at most one strong one). This is the market
   research phase.
3. **Production method** — animator partner, paid freelancer, or AI-assisted
   pipeline; storyboards stay in-house either way.
4. **Monetization thesis beyond AdSense** — since Shorts RPM alone won't hit
   revenue goals at low millions of views.
